1 · Candlestick Fundamentals DECK 1

Anatomy → chart types → reading a single candle

BULL Anatomy of a candle

Real body = distance between open & close. Upper/lower shadows (wicks) = the high and low reached during the period.

Green (bullish): close > open — buyers won the period. Red (bearish): close < open — sellers won.

Big vs. small bodies

A large body = greater distance traveled = stronger momentum. A small body = buyers/sellers were only slightly stronger — weak conviction.

Long wicks on one side

A candle with a long bottom wick = price was pushed down then rejected → strong buying pressure. A long top wick = strong selling pressure.

01 Candlestick reversal & continuation patterns

▸

Engulfing pattern — two candles where a smaller first candle is fully engulfed by a larger candle of the opposite color. The bigger the second candle's body relative to the first, the stronger the reversal signal.

3-bar continuation pattern — a sequence of three same-colored candles that must form in order. The bigger the average candle body, the more reliable the continuation.

3-bar reversal pattern — this pattern consists of a sequence of three candles: the 1st candle is large with a mostly full body, the 2nd candle's body is smaller but still same color, and the 3rd candle is large with a full body of the opposite color.

Rule of thumb: if the 3rd (reversal) candle's body is smaller than the 1st candle's body → lower success rate. If it's equal or larger → higher success rate.

Breakout candles — a pattern made of multiple small consolidation candles followed by one large, decisive candle that closes the range and pushes price significantly outward (up = bullish, down = bearish).

Shrinking candles — at least three consecutive candles getting progressively smaller, followed by a large candle in the opposite direction — this suggests the trend is losing steam and a reversal is confirmed once the large opposite candle appears.

Pin bar — a one-candle pattern with a small body sitting on one side and a long wick on the other, signaling rejection of that price level.

Doji — a very small body (or none at all), showing indecision between buyers and sellers: price swung up and down but closed near where it opened, meaning neither side gained ground.

02 Chart types

▸

Chart families covered: Line, Candlestick, Columns (bar), Area, High-Low, Heikin Ashi, Renko, Line Break, Kagi, and others.

  • Line chart — the most basic finance chart; plots only the closing price over time.
  • Candlestick chart — the workhorse of technical analysis; shows open, high, low, and close for every period.
Line and candlestick charts are the two most frequently used chart types in practice.

03 Trends

▸
  • Uptrend — higher highs and higher lows.
  • Downtrend — lower highs and lower lows.
  • Sideways / range-bound — highs and lows stay within a band.
Never sell in an uptrend. Never buy in a downtrend. Never trade a sideways market — wait and watch for the direction of the breakout.

04 Support & resistance

▸

Support — a price level where a decline tends to pause or bounce.

Resistance — a price level where an advance tends to pause or reverse.

Support and resistance are usually the first structural levels traders mark before layering in patterns or indicators.

05 Indicators

▸

MACD (Moving Average Convergence/Divergence) — a trend/momentum indicator built from two moving averages (the MACD line and a signal line).

Thumb rule: MACD line crosses the signal line from above → SELL. MACD line crosses the signal line from below → BUY.

RSI (Relative Strength Index) — a momentum oscillator (0–100) used to gauge overbought/oversold conditions.

Thumb rule: RSI > 60 → lean buy. RSI < 40 → lean sell. RSI between 40–60 → stand aside, no clear edge.

06 Chart patterns

▸

Cup & Handle — a continuation pattern: price rounds out a "cup" shape, forms a small "handle" pullback near the rim, then breaks out toward a measured target.

Double Top / Double Bottom — a reversal pattern: price tests a level twice, fails to break through, and reverses through the "neckline" formed between the two peaks/troughs.

2 · Market Structure DECK 2

Reading swings, trend transitions, breakouts, liquidity & fractals

Why structure matters

Understanding structure lets you: identify trends and trend transitions, spot specific trade opportunities, place correct stop-loss / take-profit levels, remove the mystery of how price moves, and extract order from apparent chaos.

The market's three behaviors

Every market, in every timeframe, is always doing one of three things: trending up, trending down, or moving sideways. Price action produces the same behaviors on every timeframe — the timeframe you trade doesn't change the underlying structure.

07 Dow Theory: the three trend types

▸
  • Uptrend — a repeating sequence of Higher Highs (HH) and Higher Lows (HL).
  • Downtrend — a repeating sequence of Lower Highs (LH) and Lower Lows (LL).
  • Sideways — "flat" highs and "flat" lows that stay within a similar range.

08 Supply & demand behind each trend

▸
TrendSupply vs. Demand
UptrendSupply < Demand
DowntrendSupply > Demand
SidewaysSupply ≈ Demand
Newton's First Law, applied to markets: "A trend will continue until a major force disrupts it." Trading with the trend is easier than anticipating a reversal — but you still need to be prepared for the reversal when it comes.

09 The four ways price reverses

▸
  • Failure swing / pullback — in an uptrend, price makes a Lower High (LH) instead of a new Higher High — the first crack in trend momentum.
  • Non-failure swing / expanding pivot (trap) — price makes one more Higher High, but then breaks below the prior Higher Low, printing a Lower Low and flipping the structure.
  • Double top / bottom — price forms a Higher High, pulls back, then fails to exceed that high (a "flat high") before breaking the prior low.
  • V-pattern / climax top — a sharp, single-swing reversal straight off a fresh Higher High with no gradual topping process.

10 Reading candle bodies & shadows within structure

▸

The relationship between highs, lows, candle bodies, and shadows changes how you interpret structure — specifically whether a breakout is true or false, and whether the structure is solid or fake.

True breakout = price closes above the prior high (not just wicks through it). A close back inside the range after poking through is the signature of a false breakout.

How to identify momentum loss: (1) a lower low/lower high forms against the trend, (2) candles shrink in size and travel a shorter distance each bar, (3) wide swings appear after tight price movement, (4) a candle color change appears at the extreme (e.g., a red candle failing to close higher during an uptrend).

How to identify momentum gain: candles grow larger and travel a greater distance per bar in the trend's direction — trade with that gain of momentum, not against it.

11 Bull/bear traps & breakout vs. pullback trading

▸

Bull trap — price pushes above a visible level, draws in breakout buyers, then reverses hard. Bear trap — the mirror image to the downside.

Trading pure breakouts is flagged as a bad idea: breakouts are easy prey for market manipulation and are imprecise. Breakout traders tend to buy right as smart money is selling into the move, and vice versa.

Breakout trader — buys at the break, placing stops below the breakout candle → larger stop, smaller target, easy to manipulate out of the trade.

Pullback trader — waits for a retracement back toward the broken level before entering → smaller stop, larger target, harder for the market to manipulate out of the trade. Technical analysis, in this framework, is really about enhancing the location and timing of trades.

12 Liquidity & market depth

▸

Liquidity — the ease with which a market can be traded without causing significant changes in price.

Market depth — the number of resting orders across price levels. The deeper the market, the greater the liquidity.

Clusters of buy-stop orders sit just above swing highs; clusters of sell-stop orders sit just below swing lows — these clusters are pools of higher liquidity. Bull/bear trap patterns take advantage of that liquidity right above highs and right below lows to manipulate less-informed market participants in a subtle way.

13 Fractal market structure

▸

Price is fractal — the same swing patterns repeat inside (or outside) themselves depending on the timeframe you view them from. This is why the timeframe you personally trade doesn't matter: the underlying behaviors are identical across timeframes, and you can read what's happening on adjacent timeframes without switching charts.

Paradox of fractal flows: depending on your perspective (timeframe), price can be going up and down simultaneously — a "major flow" on a higher timeframe can contain a "minor flow" moving the opposite direction. Recognizing this is framed as an opportunity, not a contradiction: more powerful reversals tend to happen when the major and minor flows align in the same direction.

Beyond Candlesticks Steve Nison

Concepts from the sequel to Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques

Why this book matters

Steve Nison is widely credited as the person who introduced Japanese candlestick charting to Western traders in his first book, Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques. Beyond Candlesticks is his follow-up, extending past single-candle patterns into a set of non-time-series Japanese charting styles that were, at the time, still largely unknown outside Japan — and into how to blend candlesticks with Western tools like moving averages, RSI, and MACD.

Pattern Gallery Part I — The Patterns

Original diagrams illustrating the candle patterns Nison catalogs

Diagrams below are drawn fresh for this guide to illustrate the shapes and rules — not scans or reproductions of the book's figures.

Single candle lines

🔨 Hammer

Small body near the top of the range, little or no upper wick, and a lower wick at least twice the body's length. Appears after a decline — sellers pushed price down hard, but buyers reclaimed almost all of it by the close.

🙃 Hanging Man

Identical shape to the Hammer — small body, long lower wick — but it forms after an advance, not a decline. Same silhouette, opposite context, opposite implication: a warning that buyers are losing control.

Nison's point: the pattern's shape never changes meaning on its own — where it appears in the trend is what tells you which pattern you're looking at.

☄️ Shooting Star

The upside-down Hammer shape — small body near the bottom, long upper wick, little/no lower wick. Forms after an advance; price rallied intraday then was rejected and closed near its open, a mirror of the Hammer's rejection logic.

➕ Doji

Open and close are virtually identical. Nison treats the Doji as a pure indecision signal — its importance rises sharply after a long, mature trend or a series of tall real bodies, and shrinks in a quiet, range-bound market where indecision is unremarkable.

🌊 High-Wave Candle

A small body with unusually long upper and lower shadows — a more extreme version of indecision than a plain Doji. Price was pulled hard in both directions and went nowhere, which Nison reads as a sign that the crowd has lost conviction about direction.

📦 Accumulation / distribution

Not a single shape but a concept: a cluster of small real bodies trading in a tight range after a trend. Nison frames this as the market quietly building a new position (accumulation near a bottom, distribution near a top) before the next directional move.

Dual candle lines

☁️ Dark Cloud Cover

A tall bullish candle, then a bearish candle that opens above the prior high and closes more than halfway down into the first candle's body. The deeper that close cuts into day one, the stronger the bearish reversal reading.

☀️ Piercing Pattern

The bullish mirror of Dark Cloud Cover: a tall bearish candle, then a bullish candle that opens below the prior low and rallies to close more than halfway up into the first candle's body.

Nison's distinction from an Engulfing pattern: here the second candle closes inside the first candle's body, not beyond it.

🟩 Bullish Engulfing

A small bearish body is completely swallowed by a larger bullish body that opens lower and closes higher than the first candle's entire range. Nison notes it carries more weight after a well-established downtrend than in a choppy market.

🟥 Bearish Engulfing

The inverse: a small bullish body is engulfed by a larger bearish body. Nison also describes a "Last Engulfing" variant — the same shape appearing at the tail end of a move, used as an exit signal rather than a fresh entry.

🔲 Harami

The inverse silhouette of an Engulfing pattern — a small body sits entirely inside the prior candle's range. Reads as the trend suddenly running out of energy, though Nison treats it as a milder signal than Engulfing or the Star patterns.

✝️ Harami Cross

A Harami where the second candle is a Doji rather than just a small body. Nison ranks this as a notably stronger reversal warning than a standard Harami, since it combines "contained inside the prior range" with outright indecision.

Three-candle lines

🌅 Morning Star

Three candles: a tall bearish body, a small-bodied "star" that gaps to a new low, then a tall bullish body that closes well back up into the first candle. A three-candle bottom reversal — the star is the moment control changes hands.

🌆 Evening Star

The top-reversal mirror of the Morning Star: a tall bullish body, a small star that gaps to a new high, then a tall bearish body closing back down into the first candle.

Nison flags the Doji Star variant (morning or evening) — when the middle candle is a true Doji instead of just a small body — as a higher-conviction version of either pattern.

🪟 Windows & gapping patterns

Nison's "window" is simply a Western price gap. A few variants he covers: the Rising/Falling Window (a gap in the trend's direction, treated as a support/resistance zone on future pullbacks), Three Windows (three consecutive gaps — often a sign a trend is overextended), Two Black Gapping Candles (two bearish candles gapping lower after a top), and the Gapping Doji (a Doji that gaps away from the prior close, flagging a likely reversal once the gap is filled).

🧱 Kagi charts

A reversal-based charting style that ignores time entirely. A Kagi line only changes direction once price reverses by a meaningful, pre-set amount — thin and thick lines are used to distinguish strength, making it easier to see when a trend has genuinely lost control versus just paused.

🧱 Renko charts

Built from uniform "bricks" that are only added once price moves a fixed amount in one direction, regardless of how much time passes. Renko strips out the minor noise that clutters normal candlestick charts, which makes larger trend shifts easier to spot visually.

🧱 Three-Line Break charts

A new "line" only prints once price breaks above/below the highs or lows of the prior several lines. Because it takes a real, multi-bar move to flip direction, it's designed to filter out short-lived reversals and keep traders aligned with the dominant trend.

📐 Disparity Index

A momentum tool that measures how far the current price has stretched away from a chosen moving average, expressed as a percentage. Nison presents it as a faster, more precise cousin of the moving-average crossover — useful for spotting overextended moves before they snap back.

◆ Merging candlesticks with Western technicals

▸

A central theme of the book: candlestick signals are strongest when layered with Western tools rather than used alone — for example, treating a candlestick reversal pattern that lands exactly on a moving average, a Fibonacci level, or an RSI/MACD signal as higher-confidence than the same pattern appearing with no supporting context.

This directly parallels the "confluence" idea from your Market Structure deck — a signal that lines up with multiple independent tools (structure + pattern + indicator) is treated as more trustworthy than any single signal on its own.

◆ How this extends your workshop material

▸
  • Your candlestick deck focuses on classic reversal/continuation patterns (engulfing, 3-bar patterns, pin bars, doji) — Nison's later book adds alternative chart construction methods (Kagi, Renko, Three-Line Break) that change how price is displayed in the first place, independent of which patterns you're hunting for.
  • Where your Market Structure deck teaches you to filter signals through trend + liquidity + confirmation, Nison's Disparity Index gives you a quantitative way to flag when price has stretched too far from "fair value" and a pullback/reversal is statistically more likely.
  • Both sources agree on the same core discipline: no single candle, pattern, or indicator should be traded in isolation — context (trend, structure, and a second confirming tool) is what turns a pattern into a trade.

◆ Candles & the overall technical picture

▸

Beyond the pattern catalog, a full chapter of the book is devoted to actually trading candle signals responsibly. The recurring themes:

  • Where a candle appears matters more than the candle itself. A Hammer at all-time highs isn't a bottom signal — the same shape only means something in the context of a prior move. This is Nison's version of "location, location, location."
  • Stops and risk/reward come from the pattern's own geometry. Nison generally places protective stops just beyond the extreme of the pattern (e.g., below a Hammer's low, above a Shooting Star's high) — the pattern that generates the signal also defines where it's proven wrong.
  • Trend context first, pattern second. A reversal pattern is only meaningful if there's an established trend for it to reverse. The same candles appearing inside a sideways chop carry far less weight.
  • "Becoming a market chameleon" — Nison's phrase for staying flexible rather than mechanically forcing every chart into a named pattern. Candles are a visual aid for reading crowd psychology, not a rigid rulebook to apply blindly.
  • When to offset (exit) a trade gets the same pattern-based treatment as entries — an opposing candle signal is itself a legitimate reason to close a position, not just a reason to open a new one.
This mirrors the "confirmation before entry" discipline in your Market Structure deck almost exactly — Nison is essentially describing the same location + confirmation logic, just from the candlestick side of the toolkit.
